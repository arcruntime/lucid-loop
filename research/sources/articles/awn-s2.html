Riot Games and Fortiche Push Every Possible Boundary in ‘Arcane’ Season 2

Alex Yee, Jason Chan, Arnaud-Loris Baudry, and Julien Georgel take a deep dive into the expansive and challenging visual development – concept art, background designs, character designs, props and colorscripts – that helped turn a battle royale game into a stunning, compelling, and emotionally driven Netflix series.

By Trevor Hogg | Friday, May 16, 2025 at 4:43pm
In 2D, 3D, Art, CG, Digital Media, Games, People, Television, Visual Effects | ANIMATIONWorld | Geographic Region: All

In many ways, a video game players’ point of view is extremely limited. For many games, that focus is solely on annihilating the opposition rather than getting to know different characters and exploring various environments on a battlefield that resembles an open world chessboard. Such is the case with League of Legends, an online battle royale created by Riot Games. Which made the game company’s decision to produce an animated series that allowed for a wider perspective and more creative opportunities to enrich the franchise such an incredibly big and challenging project.

That series, Arcane, debuted on Netflix in 2021, quickly captivating audiences with its stunning visuals and emotionally gripping storytelling. Inspired by Riot Games’ League of Legends, the show quickly became a cultural phenomenon on its way to numerous awards, including a whopping four Primetime Emmy Awards and nine Annie Awards, among other accolades. The second and final season debuted this past November 9, pushing out even further with its highly stylized animation design and compelling storytelling that led up to an utterly satisfying finale.

“The thing that Christian Linke [Co-Creator, Showrunner and Executive Producer, Arcane, Riot Games] wanted to deliver to the players for a frustratingly long amount of time was a high-quality investigation, or immersion, into this world because during the game, you’re focused more on playing with your team and defeating the enemy,” explains Alex Yee, Co-Creator, Screenwriter and Executive Producer, Arcane, Riot Games.  “As Christian would say, the Arcane animation screentest was one of the first times we even saw our characters talking because we never had been at their eye level to see their face emoting.  In a lot of ways, this was delivered to players what had been a fantasy they could only have in their heads as they would read backstories and piece things together in the game.  We had an embarrassment of riches when it came to this new world, and Arcane offered a test case of how we could depict that world and try to keep the heightened pop, punk fiction aspect while delivering a narrative over the top of it.”

In the show, the character Jinx gets to play the role of big sister to an idolizing protégé named Ishla.  “What we used to say was Jinx never got the opportunity to be the big sister,” observes Yee.  “What drove her so hard when she was young was that feeling of wanting to keep up, to measure up to Vi.  We wanted to turn that on its head a bit in Season 2 and see Vi sink and Jinx rise.  For Jinx to then realize the weight that has always been a part of Vi. The hard thing for Jinx to understand was that she saw all the things that happened to her through the lens of someone who wasn’t calling the shots and was getting batted around.  In Season 2, getting to understand the mantle that Vi had carried the whole time in some ways provided an avenue of forgiveness, or at least an avenue of understanding between the two of them.” 

The emotional journey of the estranged siblings is reflected in their hairstyles. “Jinx actually goes through fewer changes to her look,” notes Jason Chan, Senior Principal Visual Development Artist, Riot Games.  When she cuts her hair near the end, that represents a pivotal change in her character.  Jinx is trying to cut off the cycle of violence that she is trapped in. There are less extreme versions of that happening with other characters as well, like during her down moments, Vi has dyed her hair black.  It’s the most angry, despairing part of her arc.  As Vi comes out of it, that dark dye fades a bit too. Caitlyn as well goes through different hairstyle changes, from a really put together ponytail look for when she’s ‘put together’ to it becomes long when she’s literally letting her hair down and letting go a little bit.  Caitlyn actually ends up with a different ponytail than she started with.  It’s subtle. But we played with lots of these little things.”   

New characters were created for the project. “Mel Medarda and Silco were not in the game, and we leaned more heavily into that out of a desire to expand the world by stepping outside of what players were familiar with,” explains Yee.  “Also, we wanted to see what it would look like for characters in this world to rule without having that combat power.  Of course, we led them to places where they changed.  Early on we felt that it would make the world feel smaller if there were too many champions everywhere.  Mel was someone we wanted as part of a broader cast in Piltover; she also had a magnetism in the first concept art that one of our artists did for her. If you looked behind the scenes, we took a pause during Season 1. During that time, there were a bunch of characters that we had created and wondered if our story was getting too big. After that, Mel’s story came more to the fore. It’s great because it feels like something that Arcane is giving back to the game and allows people to participate in that way.  Mel is a great addition to the overall cast.”    

Distinctly separating and unifying the various universes was the way in which magic was created and utilized.  “The way the world building team created this planet Runeterra is that all the regions had a different affinity towards magic,” explains Arnaud-Loris Baudry, Production Designer and Senior Concept Artist, Riot Games.  “The idea of Piltover would be magic through science.  Zaun would be magic through chemicals, even if it’s toxic.  In Episode 108, we discover Mel’s mother, Ambessa, coming with her warship [from Noxus]. We wanted it to feel like it’s a clash of style with this dark grey and red because she’s coming from an almost different universe where they embrace magic completely differently.  In Season 2 you see the type of magic that they are using with the Black Rose.  The style of the visual effects is totally different because we also wanted to create a clash with what we have seen from Hextech and Chemtech. It’s our version of black magic.” 

Despite the fantasy elements, the story still had to feel grounded. “In the first Star Wars, you’re on a planet and one detail shows you it’s a science fiction piece [the twin suns],” states Baudry.  “A mix of something grounded and unique to the IP is great.  For our case, we had enough time to build the city completely in 3D and have a clear map.  But we liked this idea that the city would almost be like a theatre set. We wanted to show that in each big establishing shot.  We didn’t want everything to actually make sense in terms of scale. For example, if you are faraway, one of the towers is a kilometer high, but it’s more the silhouette of the city that we wanted to use.  Part of our job was defining virtual anchors, so the audience did not feel completely lost.” 

Animation was kept in mind when designing the characters. “Sometimes we would be like, ‘It would be great if there’s a bunch of fidgety little things that move all over,’” remarks Chan.  “We did that with Vi’s mechanical gauntlets, or Smidge who has a full crazy body rig that has so many minute parts spinning around.  Or even in some of the props. Near the end of the series, Caitlyn Kiramman has this rifle where every little piece of it moves around and is animated, which is probably a lot harder for the animators. I was specific on how it should work. But it gave the animators some toys to play with so they could accentuate things when they wanted to.  The same with the gauntlet. There are all these flaps and things that shoot out steam. But the animators could play around with them to make it feel cool.  Other times we had to make sure we didn’t limit movement too much, like if the armor was too big and bulky or got in the way of joints. There was definitely a back-and-forth process to make sure that everything worked.” 

Throughout Season 2, the character designs reflect the emotional and narrative arc of the storytelling.  “Caitlyn starts with her normal purple blue dress, which existed from the first season,” states Chan. “Then she comes into her own power. Now she’s in this slightly more tactical uniform when she’s trying to assert her power and tries to take matters in her own hands. It’s like literally what is happening in the show and also represents where she’s going as a character. After that she goes into her dark authoritarian leader arc.  Usually, Caitlyn is a thin silhouette. But suddenly, she has this cape and a much more imposing silhouette.  After that she starts to come back to herself, and we lose the cape.  By the end, when Caitlyn is reconciling with Vi and they have their romantic moment, she’s stripped off all of the armor and uniform.  It’s more normal clothes.  She has a turtleneck and pants that are still nicely fit and styled but bring her down to a relatable person.  When we go into the final battle, Caitlyn is suited up for war again. Everything is darker and more serious. There is that lasting imprint from training under Ambessa.  Even though you go through a dark period it’s not like you come out and lose that darkness.  You reconcile everything together.”    

Also reflecting the narrative arcs are the color palette and lighting schemes.  “A considerable effort has been made to harmonize the aesthetics with the direction and the story, both in terms of individual scenes and of the narrative arcs and the series as a whole, including Season 1,” notes Julien Georgel, Art Director, Fortiche.   “We have taken care to connect all the relevant aspects for an immersive and coherent experience.  The major narrative elements such as the manifestations of the Arcane [the Hexcore, the Anomaly and its corruption, the magical powers] have a significant influence on our color palette and lighting, contributing to the unique atmosphere of each sequence.  Viktor’s commune and Viktor’s cosmos realm state, which we called the Hexzone, were rooted in a key scene from Season 1: Viktor's drop of blood infusing into the Hexcore. We developed this imagery by combining geometric lines reminiscent of constellations with the organic nature of Viktor's blood. This fusion, materialized by a spiral fractal pattern, is intended as a visual representation of the mysteries of existence. It could be interpreted as an exploration of the unknown, a dive into the meanderings of consciousness.

Numerous scenes take place in the labs of various scientists.  “Jayce and Viktor are actually partners and the idea was that their lab should feel like a vault to protect them because they’re going to do a lot of dangerous things,” remarks Baudry.  “It was Julien Georgel’s idea that it would have fake daylight rather than a real window.  I created a lot of vent systems because the area would be flooded with poisonous gas and toxic waste and maybe with water from their experiments. The ground could be like a big drainage system.  And some moving parts are ways to protect themselves if they are afraid that something could explode.  We also wanted each of the scientists to have their own area. Their room is divided into two areas.  We were trying to foreshadow their future inventions. For example, Viktor is creating a claw that has a laser. That’s actually an ability that he has in game. At first it was an Easter egg, but we thought maybe it could be literally what he will get at the end of the show. We didn’t know how his experiments would go.  But it was still fun to show that because the fans will understand what it is.  For Jayce, it was more his invention of Hextech and the portal.” 

12 landmarks were designed so that viewers would not get disoriented regarding where the action was taking place in Piltover and Zaun. Progress Bridge became the centerpiece. “We took quite a bit of time designing Progress Bridge,” says Baudry.   “In New York, you often have some geometrical statues and the directors at Fortiche were thinking that something similar could be the witness of the entire story. You have some shots of pillars on this bridge where it feels like they are actually witnessing the events.  Another big one was Academic Square. It’s a place of science. It’s where Heimerdinger has his labs and where the big breakthroughs are done. That’s where Jinx is stealing the Hextech. We have a few that we always see in the environment.  We designed two cable car systems to go to the undercity [Zaun]. At some point one was used in Episode 104 when Vi goes to the undercity. Caitlyn actually bought the ticket and Vi is so happy to be free. There is a parkour scene where Vi is actually jumping to the undercity and not taking the cable car.  Another one is the clock tower. The place feels cohesive because of these few landmarks.”    

Regarding art direction, the biggest challenge was the sheer number of elements associated with Hexcore and the Anomaly. “Characters like Viktor and his community who transform into those automatons, props like Jayce’s hammer, multiple backgrounds, visual effects, and layout/framing with the obsessive spiral pattern had to work by themselves and be connected to this common pattern that has some kind of cosmic origins,” states Georgel.  “Several artists, both on Riot’s and Fortiche’s side, were working on those elements, which was difficult but fun!   We also tried to bring the viewers into new moods and environments. We obviously have a lot of dark and sad moments after the explosion of Jinx’s rocket and its consequences. But we’ve also brought some surprises and some rhythm in terms of moods, so it’s not all darkness and war.  The ‘cosmic’ aspect of some major elements and moments in the season was a nice challenge to work on.” 

Deviating from the painterly animation style are the musical sequences that harken back to the music videos that Fortiche has made over the years for League of Legends.  “They provided the artistic team at Fortiche with many opportunities to explore a variety of visual approaches,” explains Georgel. “Anne-Laure To supervised the sequences of Episodes 202, 203 and 204, due to their similarity to her style. I directed the charcoal sequence of Episode 201 [drawn by Kevin Lemoigne and myself], the visions and memories of Vander/Warwick [Episodes 204 and 205], the mental universe of Viktor [the Hexzone] and the watercolor sequence of childhood memories [Episode 206], entirely painted by Éléa Gobbé-Mévellec.” 

Being accessible was the hardest aspect to achieve on the show.  “We wanted it to be a show that all of our players could experience,” Yee shares.  “Trying to have mature themes that we wanted to talk about but also keeping it at a level where it wasn’t becoming gated by a rating in different regions.  We wanted this to be a show like the game that could be available to kids of that age.  We needed to be more methodical in how we approached the tone.  But in the beginning, it was tricky.  There was this watershed moment for me when I was thinking specifically about some anime because it had mature themes but was still light and had an epic feel.  It’s something that you would see more of in the action oriented or younger animation.  Once we started trying to hit that kind of sweet spot I was thinking, like Samurai Champloo or Cowboy Bebop.  The first couple lines of dialogue were a domino toppling others.  After that it was wanting to dig into a lot of big topics.”

Trevor Hogg is a freelance video editor and writer best known for composing in-depth filmmaker and movie profiles for VFX Voice, Animation Magazine, and British Cinematographer.
